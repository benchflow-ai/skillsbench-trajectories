    14→1969). The same result follows from the fact the timelike geodesic of a test particle would turn null
    15→if it would ever arrive at an event horizon (Mitra, FPL, 2000, 2002). Non-occurrrence of trapped

    61→The Weyl equation describes chiral massless relativistic particles, called Weyl fermions, which
    62→have important relations to neutrinos. A direct observation of the dynamics of Weyl fermions in an
    63→experiment is difficult to achieve. This study investigates a method of simulating the Weyl equation
    64→in 1+2-dimension by a single trapped ion. The predictions about a two-dimensional Zitterbewegung
    65→and an especially interesting phenomenon of Weyl fermions can be tested by the future trapped ion
    66→experiment, which might enhance our understanding of neutrinos.

    71→tiny mass; hence, the Weyl equation can be used to approximately describe neutrinos. In the Standard Model
    72→of particle physics (SM), neutrinos are assumed to be
    73→massless, and only left-handed neutrinos exist [1]. The
    74→following remains an open question: why do left-handed
    75→neutrinos exist, but not right-handed neutrinos? Neutrinos do not have an electric charge, and are difficult
    76→to detect. Moreover, its properties are still not exactly
    77→clear. An analog quantum simulation [2–6] of the Weyl

    80→controllable systems to simulate systems that are hard

    83→[9]; and quantum field theory is simulated using trapped
    84→ions[10, 11]. The Zitterbewegung phenomenon, which is

    88→has been discussed by [14].

   180→remains a crucial challenge. Here, we propose and analyze a new strategy to enhance the coherent interactions
   181→in trapped ion systems via parametric amplification of the ions’ motion—by squeezing the collective motional
   182→modes (phonons), the spin-spin interactions they mediate can be significantly enhanced. We illustrate the power
   183→of this approach by showing how it can enhance collective spin states useful for quantum metrology, and how
   184→it can improve the speed and fidelity of two-qubit gates in multi-ion systems, important ingredients for scalable
   185→trapped ion quantum computation. Our results are also directly relevant to numerous other physical platforms
   186→in which spin interactions are mediated by bosons.

   189→computers [1], quantum simulators [2], and quantum measurement devices [3]. For example, universal quantum gate

   192→[6] and even hundreds [7] of ions. For all of these applications, the general approach is to identify a qubit, i.e., two
   193→metastable atomic states, and then engineer interactions between qubits by controllably coupling them to the ions’ collective motion (phonons), typically using lasers [1, 8] or magnetic field gradients [9, 10]. Putting aside the details of what

   318→principles which has puzzled physicists for over forty years. The crux of the problem lies in
   319→an assumption about the structure of entanglement across the event horizon, namely, that the
   320→Hilbert space factorizes. While valid in quantum mechanics, this fails drastically in quantum
   321→field theory, and hence a deeper understanding of entanglement is required if further progress
   322→is to be made. Recently, ideas from algebraic quantum field theory have provided new insight

   328→In 1973, Jacob Bekenstein observed [1] that black holes must be endowed with an entropy
   329→in order to preserve the second law of thermodynamics; otherwise, one could decrease the
   330→entropy of the universe by simply throwing subsystems with high entropy into a black hole.
   331→At face value, this is an intuitive proposal: since the information about the degrees of freedom
   332→that comprise the hypothetical subsystem would then be hidden behind the event horizon, it
   333→makes sense to count them among the microstates of the black hole.

   475→We construct charged asymptotically flat black hole solutions in Einstein-MaxwellWeyl(EMW) gravity. These solutions can be interpreted as generalizations of two different
   476→groups: Schwarzschild black hole (SBH) and non-Schwarzschild black hole (NSBH) solutions.
   477→In addition, we discuss the thermodynamic properties of two groups of numerical solutions
   478→in detail, and show that they obey the first law of thermodynamics.
