    11→The model of facilitated diffusion describes how DNA-binding proteins, such as
    12→transcription factors (TFs), find their chromosomal targets by combining 3D diffusion
    13→through the cytoplasm and 1D sliding along nonspecific DNA sequences. The redundant
    14→1D diffusion near the specific binding site extends the target size and facilitates target
    15→location. While this model successfully predicts the kinetics measured in test tubes, it has not
    16→been extended to account for the highly crowded environment in living cells. Here, we
    17→investigate the effect of other DNA-binding proteins that partially occupy the bacterial
    18→chromosome. We show how they would slow down the search process, mainly through
    19→restricted sliding near the target. This implies that increasing the overall DNA-binding
    20→protein concentration would have a marginal effect in reducing the search time, because any
    21→additional proteins would restrict the search process for each other. While the presence of
    22→other proteins prevents sliding from the flanking DNA, DNA looping provides an
    23→alternative path to transfer from neighboring sites to the target efficiently. We propose that,
    24→when looping is faster than the initial search process, the auxiliary binding sites further
    25→extend the effective target region and therefore facilitate the target location.

    27→Introduction
    28→
    29→DNA-binding proteins control both when and how the genome functions. Moreover, they limit how fast
    30→the genomic information can be accessed. For instance, the rate at which a transcription factor (TF) finds its
    31→specific binding site determines how rapidly the corresponding gene can be turned on or off. When this rate is
    32→low compared to the speed of the regulated process, the binding and unbinding kinetics of the protein may be
    33→more important for the overall system behavior than its often well-characterized equilibrium properties.
    34→Although seemingly a simple process, target location by DNA-binding proteins requires scanning through
    35→millions to billions of similar nonspecific DNA sequences. It was hypothesized [1, 2] and later demonstrated [3]
    36→that DNA-binding proteins partly diffuse (slide) along the nonspecific DNA while scanning for the specific site
    37→(Fig. 1). The process of alternating 3D diffusion in cytoplasm and 1D diffusion along DNA is called facilitated
    38→target location [4]. Originally discovered in vitro, this mechanism facilitates the process of target location by
    39→extending the target size via efficient sliding from neighboring nonspecific DNA, while taking advantage of
    40→three-dimensional diffusion to carry the molecule over large distances. The complexity of this search process
    41→has attracted both theoretical and experimental attention over the past few decades [2, 5-14].
    42→In a living cell, however, the target location is further complicated by the vast amount of other proteins,
    43→both cytoplasmic and DNA-bound. More than 50% of the dry weight of an E. coli cell is protein [15]. The 3D
    44→diffusion in the cytoplasm is slowed down by at least one order of magnitude compared to that in water.
    45→Furthermore, more than 100,000 proteins are associated with DNA per cell, mostly bound nonspecifically [16,
    46→17]. As a result, the bacterial chromosome is at least 20-30% occupied, with mostly uniformly distributed short
    47→naked DNA fragments [18]. The sliding motion along DNA must be hindered as well. Therefore, for facilitated
    48→target location, it is important to consider the effects of these macromolecules that are absent in the in vitro
    49→settings.
    50→In the current paper we adapt the theory for facilitated target location to the in vivo situation, with
    51→emphasis on the crowding on chromosomal DNA in bacterial cells. First, the high occupancy by other proteins
    52→results in roadblocks which restrict the open sliding on DNA. The effect is analyzed for both stationary
    53→roadblocks and sliding roadblocks. Second, the other proteins compete for both nonspecific sites and specific
    54→site with the target locator. We combine these effects and derive analytical expressions for how the rates of
    55→binding to a specific site are reduced by roadblocks. We also propose that DNA looping from auxiliary sites
    56→can bypass the roadblocks and speed up the search process. Specific examples will be given with the E. coli
    57→lactose utilization (lac) operon, the paradigm of gene regulation. We focus on the lac repressor (LacI), which

   502→DNA is a highly refined nanomechanical object. The
   503→interplay between strong covalent bonds of the backbone
   504→and weak hydrogen interactions between bases [1], the
   505→thermal bath in which it is immersed, and the proximity
   506→of physiological conditions to the denaturation temperature, make DNA highly and non-linearly responsive to
   507→mechanical and thermal changes, and render any solely
   508→mechanical approach inviable. This is critical for nanotechnology, where DNA is the basis for novel materials [2], but understanding double helix thermomechanics
   509→would also illuminate biology, where enzymes involved in
   510→replication and repair are viewed as molecular motors.
   511→In the past twenty years, direct single molecule manipulation [3, 4] has revolutionized our understanding of key
   512→aspects of DNA, revealing new couplings and transitions
   513→between different structures, whose nature and forms,
   514→however, are still speculative. When a few micrometer
   515→long strand of DNA is stretched to a tension of the order
   516→of pico-Newtons (pNs) to avoid formation of plectonemes,
   517→sharp transitions are activated at positive and negative
   518→torques, while an overstretching transition is observed
   519→for DNA under tension of ≃ 60 pN at zero torque [5].
   520→Tentative tension–torque phase diagrams for the stability of B–DNA, and various phenomenological theories,
   521→have been proposed to explain these effects. The robustness of the Peyrard-Bishop-Dauxois approach (PBD)
   522→[6–8] has been corroborated by Cocco and Barbi [9, 10]:
   523→they incorporated torque and successfully reproduced denaturation by unwinding. However, these recent studies
   524→do not include tension, do not explain denaturation at
   525→overwinding, and do not provide phase diagrams in the
   526→tension-torque plane. Also, although much simpler than
   527→the molecular structure they describe, their complexity
   528→still calls for numerical treatments, and cannot offer analytic equations to more easily guide experiments.

   561→Can the arts be analyzed in a quantitative manner? We propose a methodology to study music development by
   562→applying multivariate statistics on composers characteristics. Seven representative composers were considered
   563→in terms of eight main musical features. Grades were assigned to each characteristic and their correlations
   564→were analyzed. A bootstrap method was applied to simulate hundreds of artificial composers influenced by the
   565→seven representatives chosen. Applying dimensionality reduction we obtained a planar space used to quantify
   566→non-numeric relations like dialectics, opposition and innovation. Composers differences on style and technique
   567→were represented as geometrical distances in the planar space, making it possible to quantify, for example,
   568→how much Bach and Stockhausen differ from other composers or how much Beethoven influenced Brahms.
   569→In addition, we compared the results with a prior investigation on philosophy1 . The influence of dialectics,
   570→strong on philosophy, was not remarkable on music. Instead, supporting an observation already considered
   571→by music theorists, strong influences were identified between subsequent composers, implying inheritance and
   572→suggesting a stronger master-disciple evolution when compared to the philosophy analysis.
