    76→Abstract
    77→Popular music is a key cultural expression that has captured listeners’ attention for
    78→ages. Many of the structural regularities underlying musical discourse are yet to be discovered and, accordingly, their historical evolution remains formally unknown. Here we
    79→unveil a number of patterns and metrics characterizing the generic usage of primary musical facets such as pitch, timbre, and loudness in contemporary western popular music.
    80→Many of these patterns and metrics have been consistently stable for a period of more
    81→than fifty years, thus pointing towards a great degree of conventionalism. Nonetheless,
    82→we prove important changes or trends related to the restriction of pitch transitions, the
    83→homogenization of the timbral palette, and the growing loudness levels. This suggests that
    84→our perception of the new would be rooted on these changing characteristics. Hence, an old
    85→tune could perfectly sound novel and fashionable, provided that it consisted of common
    86→harmonic progressions, changed the instrumentation, and increased the average loudness.

   200→A localised particle in Quantum Mechanics is described by a wave packet in position space,
   201→regardless of its energy. However, from the point of view of General Relativity, if the particle’s
   202→energy density exceeds a certain threshold, it should be a black hole. In order to combine these two
   203→pictures, we introduce a horizon wave-function determined by the particle wave-function in position
   204→space, which eventually yields the probability that the particle is a black hole. The existence of a
   205→minimum mass for black holes naturally follows, albeit not in the form of a sharp value around the
   206→Planck scale, but rather like a vanishing probability that a particle much lighter than the Planck
   207→mass be a black hole. We also show that our construction entails an effective Generalised Uncertainty
   208→Principle (GUP), simply obtained by adding the uncertainties coming from the two wave-functions
   209→associated to a particle. Finally, the decay of microscopic (quantum) black holes is also described
   210→in agreement with what the GUP predicts.

   388→Following its formation, a black hole-neutron star (BH-NS)
   389→binary system loses orbital energy and angular momentum by
   390→emitting gravitational radiation. During the inspiral stage, it

   392→reservoir is emptied and the two compact objects merge. The
   393→remnant of this coalescence consists of a black hole possibly
   394→surrounded by a hot, massive accretion torus [1]. BH-NS binaries have not been observed yet, but population synthesis

   441→Abstract
   442→We analyze the efficiency of quantum simulations of fermionic and bosonic
   443→models in trapped ions. In particular, we study the optimal time of entangling
   444→gates and the required number of total elementary gates. Furthermore, we
   445→exemplify these estimations in the light of quantum simulations of quantum field
   446→theories, condensed-matter physics, and quantum chemistry. Finally, we show
   447→that trapped-ion technologies are a suitable platform for implementing quantum
   448→simulations involving interacting fermionic and bosonic modes, paving the way for
   449→overcoming classical computers in the near future.
   450→03.67.Ac, 03.67.Lx, 37.10.Ty
   451→Keywords: Quantum Information; Quantum Simulation; Trapped Ions

   453→1 Background
   454→Quantum simulation is one of the most promising fields in quantum information
   455→science. Feynman already pointed out in 1982 [1] that a controllable quantum platform could simulate the dynamics or static properties of another quantum system
   456→exponentially faster than classical computers. Since then, this hypothesis has been
   457→demonstrated [2], and important theoretical and experimental work followed [3–5].
   458→Furthermore, quantum simulators establish analogies between previously unconnected fields, and have as a main aim to overcome classical computers.
   459→Many proposals and experimental realizations of quantum simulations in a broad
   460→variety of platforms have been put forward, as for example spin systems [6–9], the
   461→Bose-Hubbard model implemented with cold atoms [10], quantum chemistry [11]
   462→and quantum statistics [12–14] simulated with photonic systems, condensed matter
   463→models with Rydberg atoms [15], relativistic quantum mechanics [16–19], quantum field theories [20], and the lattice Schwinger model [21]. On the other hand,
   464→quantum simulations of fermionic and bosonic systems in trapped ions have been
   465→recently proposed [22]. Therefore, it is timely to study the experimental requirements needed, to assess the feasibility of the proposal and to compare it with other
   466→implementations.
   467→In this article, we analyze the necessary resources to implement a quantum simulation of fermions and bosons with trapped ions [20, 22–24]. We show that the
   468→methods developed for simulating fermionic and bosonic systems with ions can save
   469→a large amount of resources in terms of gates with respect to other platforms. This
   470→demonstrates that trapped ions are a promising quantum technology for a wide
   471→variety of quantum simulations, including high energy physics, condensed matter,
   472→or quantum chemistry.

   479→quantum information protocols. Ions are charged particles that can be confined
   480→either in Penning traps [25] or radio-frequency (rf) Paul traps [26]. The former
   481→uses electrostatic and magnetic fields, whereas the latter requires time-dependent
   482→fields to confine the ions in an effective harmonic potential. Here, we will focus on
   483→rf Paul traps, see Fig 1a. Two different kinds of qubits are currently employed,
   484→optical qubits and radio-frecuency qubits (rf qubits). In the first ones, see Fig. 1b,
   485→two internal metastable electronic levels corresponding to an optical transition are

   487→mediate a two-photon transition between the hyperfine or Zeeman electronic levels

   499→Abstract
   500→Finite-context modeling is a powerful tool for compressing and hence for representing DNA
   501→sequences. We describe an algorithm to detect genomic regularities, within a blind discovery
   502→strategy. The algorithm uses information profiles built using suitable combinations of finitecontext models. We used the genome of the fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe strain
   503→972 h− for illustration, unveiling locations of low information content, which are usually
   504→associated with DNA regions of potential biological interest.
   505→
   506→Introduction
   507→Graphical representations of DNA sequences are a handy way of quickly finding regions of potential interest. This has been a topic addressed using various approaches
   508→(see, for example, [1–10]), some of them relying on information theoretical principles.
   509→Both global and local estimates of the randomness of a sequence provide useful information, but both also have shortcomings. Global estimates do not show how the
   510→characteristics change along the sequence. Local estimates fail to take into consideration the global properties of the sequence. This latter drawback was addressed by
   511→Clift et al. [11] using the concept of sequence landscape, plots displaying the number
   512→of times oligonucleotides from the target sequence occur in a given source sequence.
   513→If the target and source sequences coincide, then the landscape provides information
   514→about self-similarities (repeats) of the target sequence.
   515→The sequence landscapes of Clift et al. [11] have been a first attempt to display
   516→local information, taking into account global characteristics of the sequence. This
   517→idea was pursed by Allison et al. [12] using XM, a model that considers a sequence as
   518→a mixture of regions with little structure and regions that are approximate repeats.
   519→With this statistical model, they have produced information sequences, which quantify the amount of surprise of having a given base at a given position, knowing the
   520→remaining of the sequence. When plotted, one of these information sequences provides
   521→a quick overview of certain properties of the original symbolic sequence, allowing for
   522→example to easily identify zones of rich repetitive content [13–15].
   523→The information sequences of Allison et al. [12] are deeply related to data compression. The role of data compression for pattern discovery in DNA sequences was
   524→initially pointed out by Grumbach et al. [16] and, since then, it has been pursued
   525→by other researchers (e.g. [13, 17]). In fact, the algorithmic information content of a
   526→sequence is the size, in bits, of the shortest description of the sequence.
   527→In this paper, we propose using combinations of several finite-context models, each
   528→of a different depth, for building information profiles. Such models have been shown
   529→
   530→to adequately capture the statistical properties of DNA sequences [18–21] but are
   531→direction-dependent, i.e., the results depend on which direction the DNA sequence
   532→is processed. We remove this directional dependency by combining the amount of
   533→information that a certain DNA base carries in each processing direction.
   534→The information profiles are found using an algorithm based on finite-context
   535→models that needs time proportional to the length of the sequence. We present a
   536→proof-of-concept study of the potential of information profiles in genome analysis,
   537→namely, for detecting genomic structural and functional regularities. We uncover
   538→genomic regularities on a large-scale, such as, centromeric and telomeric regions of
   539→a chromosome, or transposable elements. In this context, we use the genome of the
   540→fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe strain 972 h− as case-study.

   558→Abstract Black hole thermodynamics emerged from the classical general relativistic laws of black hole mechanics, summarized by Bardeen–Carter–Hawking, together with the physical insights by Bekenstein about black hole entropy and the
   559→semi-classical derivation by Hawking of black hole evaporation. The black hole
   560→entropy law inspired the formulation of the holographic principle by ’t Hooft and
   561→Susskind, which is famously realized in the gauge/gravity correspondence by Maldacena, Gubser–Klebanov–Polaykov and Witten within string theory. Moreover, the
   562→microscopic derivation of black hole entropy, pioneered by Strominger–Vafa within
   563→string theory, often serves as a consistency check for putative theories of quantum
   564→gravity. In this book chapter we review these developments over five decades, starting in the 1960ies.
